A human-centered engineering-design project built around a real client. As team **Manager**, I led a four-person team in designing **GripEase** — a lightweight grip-assisting glove with integrated wrist support for a client with severe rheumatoid arthritis. Through a formal design cycle — client interviews, objectives and constraints, a weighted decision matrix, and repeated client-validated prototyping — we delivered a **sub-100-gram, ~$50 device** that restores grip and independence for everyday tasks, engineered around the client's exact physical limitations rather than adapted from a generic product.

## The client &amp; the problem

Our client, Mark, is a 64-year-old with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis diagnosed at age 6. Decades of chronic inflammation left him with reduced grip strength, finger stiffness, and **wrist rotation limited to roughly 90°** — making everyday tasks like brushing his teeth, writing, or using utensils difficult. Existing gripping aids didn't account for his specific combination of limitations. The research gap was clear: therapy gloves address arthritis *symptoms* (swelling, pain) but fall short on *functional* grip support. Our objective was to fill exactly that gap with a purpose-built device that "works with Mark's hand rather than around it."

## Objectives, constraints &amp; results

We defined measurable objectives and constraints up front, then tested against them:

| Requirement | Target / result |
|---|---|
| Lightweight | &lt; 100 g ✓ |
| Accessible | Donnable with ≤ 90° wrist rotation ✓ |
| Durable | Survives drop testing (no failure) ✓ |
| Safe | No discomfort; does not restrict circulation ✓ |
| Simple | Usable by volunteers without instructions ✓ |
| Cost-effective | ~$50 total materials ✓ |

## Concept generation &amp; decision matrix

Every team member sketched an independent concept — a grip-assist glove, an extendable button-pusher, an "Ironman" reaching arm, and an elbow-hand rehabilitation gear system. We evaluated all four in a **weighted decision matrix** scored across seven criteria (safe, accessible, lightweight, durable, cost-effective, grips objects, rehabilitation). Combined with TA design reviews and direct client feedback, the matrix drove convergence on the **grip-assist glove** — the most general, accessible, and practical solution — merging the strongest grip and support elements from the top concepts.

## The design: GripEase

The final device is built on a **modified leather bench-press glove**, a base chosen deliberately for its built-in wrist support and grippy leather palm. Its key engineered features all trace directly to a client constraint:

- **Front-fastening straps** — the top strap closes over the back of the hand, so the user never has to rotate their wrist to put it on, directly accommodating Mark's ~90° rotation limit.
- **Bench-press-inspired wrist strap** — provides the joint stability the client specifically requested, tuned so it never cuts off circulation.
- **Leather palm** — selected over the original thin material after grip testing, for durable, high-friction hold.
- **Seven attachment points** — four hooks on top, an adjustable palm hook, and two hooks between the index finger and thumb — letting the client secure and carry objects (toothbrush, pencil, utensils) with minimal finger effort.

## Iterative prototyping &amp; client validation

The heart of the project was a tight feedback loop with the client, where each round of testing produced a concrete design change:

- **Too thin, weak grip** → switched the base material to leather for support and friction.
- **No wrist support** → added the bench-press-style wrist strap.
- **Couldn't rotate wrist to don it** → redesigned the strap system to fasten from the front.
- **Glove too large** → downsized by one inch to fit.
- **Finger supports impractical** → removed the 3D-printed finger grips after client testing.
- **Wanted more utility** → added additional hooks for general use.

Grip material itself was chosen empirically: we ran a slide test measuring how far each candidate material traveled across a surface before releasing an object — leather won while staying inexpensive.
